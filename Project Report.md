## 1. Background and Research Aims
Traffic congestion can emerge from local interactions between drivers even without an accident, lane closure, or other permanent bottleneck. On a circular road, small variations in speed can be amplified as following drivers brake more strongly than the vehicles ahead of them, eventually producing a stop-and-go wave that travels backwards despite every vehicle moving forwards. Experimental work by Sugiyama et al. (2008) demonstrated this phenomenon using human drivers on a circular track, showing that initially uniform traffic can become unstable after a small perturbation.

Chapter 10 of Think Complexity presents a simple agent-based model of this process. Vehicles travel around a single-lane circular road and select acceleration using only the distance to the vehicle ahead. Random speed errors break the initially uniform configuration, allowing persistent traffic jams to emerge as vehicle density or noise increases. Although this model illustrates emergence effectively, its drivers are identical, react immediately, and use a highly simplified acceleration rule. Real traffic instead contains drivers with different desired speeds, following distances, acceleration behaviour, perception errors, and reaction times.

Mixed traffic containing both human-driven and automated vehicles may provide a decentralised method of reducing these instabilities. Stern et al. (2018) demonstrated that controlling one vehicle in a circular-road experiment could dampen stop-and-go waves generated by more than 20 human-driven vehicles. However, the effectiveness of such control depends on assumptions about human behaviour. Li et al. (2020) found that reaction delays and behavioural heterogeneity reduce the number of human-driven vehicles that a connected automated vehicle can stabilise. Temporary incidents introduce an additional challenge because a controller must not only prevent the growth of ordinary fluctuations but also restore stable flow after a substantial disturbance.

The primary research question is therefore:
How do human reaction delay and behavioural variability affect the minimum proportion of smoothing vehicles required to suppress stop-and-go waves and restore stable traffic following a temporary incident?

The investigation has two objectives. First, it will estimate the proportion of smoothing vehicles required to maintain stable flow under different densities and human-driver characteristics. Second, it will determine how this proportion affects congestion severity and recovery time following a temporary obstruction. It is hypothesised that longer reaction delays and greater behavioural variability will increase the required proportion, while smoothing vehicles will reduce speed oscillations, hard braking, and post-incident recovery time.

## 2. Originality and Contribution
The project extends the textbook model in the following ways:
- It replaces identical, memoryless drivers with heterogeneous human agents; explicitly represents delayed perception and reaction; introduces a mixed population of human and smoothing vehicles; and evaluates resilience following a temporary incident. Thus, the contribution is therefore not simply the implementation of a "better driver." Instead, the project will systematically map how human uncertainty changes the threshold at which local smoothing behaviour stabilises the system.

The investigation also distinguishes between prevention and recovery. A traffic configuration may remain stable under small random fluctuations but fail to recover after a larger disturbance. Comparing these conditions will reveal whether the smoothing proportion required for ordinary stability is sufficient for incident resilience. The main outputs will be phase diagrams identifying stable and unstable regions, estimates of the minimum effective smoothing proportion, and measurements of recovery dynamics. These results will provide insight into how individual-level delays and control rules affect system-level traffic stability.
